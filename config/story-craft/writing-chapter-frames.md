# Writing chapter frames

The engine's default craft guidance for a chapter's **Opening**, **Setting**, and
**Closing** — the frame the `chapter-cutter` proposes around the beats `story.md`
already settled (spec `2026-08-12-chapter-setting-and-frames-design.md` §3).

This file is read through the config overlay as a **directory** — a genre pack
or a series adds files beside it, and only a file of the same name replaces it.

## What an opening does

The first sentence earns the next one. Not the room, not the weather, not what
happened since the last chapter — a concrete image or action, already in
motion.

> The kettle screamed before the potter remembered she'd filled it.

The room can arrive in the second sentence. It always can. What can't wait is
something happening.

A recap is the opposite move: it spends the earned attention on catching the
reader up rather than pulling them forward. If the last chapter's ending needs
restating, that is what the chapter *does* with it — the sleuth acting on what she
just learned — not a sentence that tells the reader she learned it.

## The three closings

A closing is not an event you invent at the boundary. It is what the chapter's
*last beat* leaves the reader holding. Three kinds, and each leaves something
different.

**Cliffhanger** — an unresolved event. Something happens, and the outcome is
withheld.

> The lock gave. Behind the door, the light was already on.

The reader wants to know what happens next because they don't yet know what
*already* happened.

**Irony** — a gap the reader can see and the character can't.

> The locksmith pocketed the key, satisfied. He hadn't noticed it was the wrong one.

The reader wants to keep reading because they now know why this is worse than
the character thinks it is. Nothing is withheld from the reader — the
withholding is from the character, and the reader feels it as dread rather
than suspense.

**Promise of action** — the character commits.

> Enough asking politely. The sleuth was going to search the shed herself,
> tonight.

Nothing is withheld and nothing is ironic; the decision itself makes the next
chapter's shape inevitable. The reader turns the page to watch the promise get
kept.

## Why a run of the same kind goes dead

Three cliffhangers in a row teach the reader something true about this book:
the withheld outcome always turns out survivable. By the fourth, the reader
has stopped paying the price a cliffhanger is supposed to charge — they know
the trick, and the ending reads as reflex rather than tension.

Variety isn't decoration for its own sake. It's what keeps each kind
expensive. A cliffhanger costs the reader worry only if the last three
chapters didn't already spend it on the same move.

## The closing belongs to the last beat

A chapter's ending is not a line added after the beats are decided — it's a
reading of the beat that's already there. If the last beat can't carry a
cliffhanger, an irony, or a commitment, the chapter's last beat is in the
wrong place, not the ending in need of invention. Move the boundary, or ask
whether the beat itself is the wrong one to end on. Writing an ending the
beats don't earn is the same mistake in reverse as a beat shaped like an
instruction to the writer (`writing-beats.md`) — both put something on the
page that describes what should happen instead of showing what does.

## Setting is where, not just when

Every beat happens somewhere, and the reader needs to know where before they
can watch it happen. A setting range says which beats share a place, time and
(when it matters) condition — a chapter that moves rooms mid-scene needs a new
range at the beat where the move happens, not a single range covering both.

Place names come from the series' own setting pack, never invented here —
this document teaches the shape of a setting note, not what any particular
book's rooms are called.
