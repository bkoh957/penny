# Four-Act Cozy Mystery Structure

> **Purpose:** A macro-structure lens for cozy mystery outlines and manuscripts.
> It describes how the investigation should change direction across the novel. It is not a
> genre checklist, chapter template, or substitute for separate cozy-archetype and
> murder-logic checks.
>
> Use it to diagnose weak **movement, escalation, act transitions, and information flow**.
> Do not force equal act lengths or map one beat to one chapter. The percentages are
> approximate pressure points, not mathematical requirements.

## Core Principle

A four-act mystery divides the long middle of a traditional three-act structure into two
opposing investigative movements:

- **Act II expands the field of possibility.**
- **Act III contracts the field of possibility.**

That distinction is the main value of the model. A mystery should not feel like one long
sequence of interviews and clues. The investigation must first generate plausible
stories, then destroy them until only the true explanation remains.

The essential movement is:

1. **Act I creates the question.**
2. **Act II multiplies the possible answers.**
3. **Act III eliminates the wrong answers.**
4. **Act IV proves the true answer and restores order.**

---

# I. Act I — The Murder Enters the Community

**Approximate range:** 0–25%

**Primary movement:** ordinary life → disruption → commitment

**Mystery function:** establish the crime, the social field, and the reason this amateur
must become involved.

**Reader question:** *What happened, and why is this sleuth the person who cannot leave it
alone?*

## 1. Establish the Protected World
<!-- job: establish-protected-world -->

Open inside the life readers are being invited to inhabit. Show the protagonist's
ordinary role, key relationships, routines, responsibilities, and distinctive way of
seeing.

The opening should establish enough pleasure, stability, aspiration, or belonging that
the murder has something meaningful to damage.

The world need not be peaceful. It does need to be legible. Readers should understand:

- what normality looks like;
- what the protagonist values;
- where they fit or fail to fit;
- what social network surrounds the coming crime;
- what part of this life could be lost.

## 2. Introduce Disturbance Before or Around the Death
<!-- job: introduce-disturbance -->

A smaller disruption often prepares the ground for murder:

- an argument;
- an unwanted visitor;
- a missing object;
- a strange delivery;
- unusual behaviour by the future victim;
- an old grievance resurfacing;
- a local event under strain;
- something subtly wrong in the protagonist's workplace or home.

This disturbance should connect the crime to the existing community rather than make the
murder feel imported solely to start the plot.

## 3. Deliver the Crime and Its First Contradiction
<!-- job: crime-and-first-contradiction -->

The central death should arrive early enough that the reader understands the book's main
promise.

The body discovery or notification should do more than announce a murder. It should also
introduce at least one destabilising detail:

- an object where it should not be;
- a broken routine;
- an apparently impossible timeline;
- a clue linked to the sleuth's work or reputation;
- a witness behaving unexpectedly;
- an official interpretation the sleuth instinctively doubts.

The contradiction need not be understood yet. Its purpose is to create investigative
pressure.

## 4. Establish the Initial Suspect Field
<!-- job: initial-suspect-field -->

Introduce the people, relationships, and tensions from which the solution will emerge.

By the end of Act I, the reader should have encountered:

- the victim's public identity;
- several people affected by the death;
- at least two or three plausible sources of conflict;
- the police or other official response;
- the first social consequences of suspicion.

The eventual killer should normally be present or meaningfully established before the
act closes.

## 5. Give the Sleuth a Compelling Reason to Investigate
<!-- job: reason-to-investigate -->

Curiosity alone is rarely enough. The protagonist should face a personal, social,
practical, or moral necessity:

- someone close to them is suspected;
- they found the body;
- the crime threatens their livelihood or reputation;
- the victim entrusted them with something;
- the police dismiss a detail they know matters;
- the murder threatens the community they are trying to join or protect;
- the evidence points toward them;
- silence would harm an innocent person.

The reason should arise from the protagonist's existing life, not from generic amateur
sleuth enthusiasm.

## 6. Act I Turning Point — Commitment
<!-- job: act-i-commitment -->

The act ends when the protagonist crosses from reaction into purposeful investigation.

The commitment may be explicit or reluctant, but it must change behaviour. After this
point, the sleuth begins seeking information, testing stories, or protecting someone in a
way that creates consequences.

The act break should answer:

> *Why does the sleuth choose to act despite the risk?*

### Act I deliverables

By the transition into Act II, the story should have established:

- a world worth caring about;
- the central crime;
- the protagonist's personal stake;
- the official theory or investigative boundary;
- the initial suspect circle;
- one or more visible contradictions;
- an active commitment to pursue the truth.

### Common Act I failures

- too much lifestyle material before the murder;
- a body with no meaningful connection to the protagonist's world;
- a sleuth who investigates only because the genre requires it;
- an eventual killer introduced too late;
- no initial contradiction or investigative question;
- an act break based on more information rather than a decision.

# II. Act II — Everyone Had a Reason

**Approximate range:** 25–50%

**Primary movement:** enquiry → expansion → uncertainty

**Mystery function:** widen the case by revealing motives, secrets, competing theories,
and misleading interpretations.

**Reader question:** *Which of these plausible stories explains the murder?*

## 7. Open the Victim's Hidden Life
<!-- job: victims-hidden-life -->

The sleuth moves beyond the victim's public identity and begins uncovering the private
stories surrounding them.

Act II should reveal:

- conflicts the town minimised;
- relationships people concealed;
- promises, debts, betrayals, or fears;
- competing versions of the victim;
- reasons several people cannot tell the full truth.

The more the sleuth learns, the less simple the murder should appear.

## 8. Expand Through Suspect Encounters
<!-- job: suspect-encounters -->

Each major investigative sequence should add a distinct possible explanation of the
crime.

A useful sequence is:

1. encounter a suspect through ordinary life or a believable obligation;
2. notice an inconsistency;
3. uncover a credible motive or opportunity;
4. form or strengthen a theory;
5. meet resistance, evasion, or social consequence;
6. gain a clue whose real meaning remains uncertain.

The scenes should not become a procession of formal interviews. Cozy investigation is
strongest when information emerges through work, friendship, errands, events, gossip,
caregiving, or community obligations.

## 9. Build Real Red Herrings
<!-- job: real-red-herrings -->

Act II is the main home of plausible false stories.

A strong red herring should contain:

- a real secret;
- a real reason to conceal it;
- evidence that appears to connect it to the murder;
- a social or emotional cost when the sleuth pushes;
- one fact that remains useful after the theory weakens.

The sleuth should often be partly right and interpretively wrong. They may identify a
lie, but misunderstand what the lie protects.

## 10. Plant the Fair-Play Solution
<!-- job: plant-fair-play-solution -->

The decisive solution should begin appearing before the midpoint, even if the reader and
sleuth assign it the wrong meaning.

Useful clue forms include:

- knowledge someone should not possess;
- a material or craft inconsistency;
- an object absent or out of place;
- an alibi that is technically true but misleading;
- language revealing expertise or prior knowledge;
- a timeline detail hidden inside routine;
- behaviour that appears emotional but is logistical;
- a clue attached to the wrong suspect or motive.

Act II should make the correct answer possible without making it obvious.

## 11. Apply the First Meaningful Pressure
<!-- job: first-meaningful-pressure -->

The consequences of investigating should become visible before the midpoint.

Pressure may include:

- a witness withdrawing cooperation;
- evidence disappearing;
- a warning or subtle threat;
- police frustration;
- damage to the sleuth's reputation;
- a suspect trying to leave;
- a friendship strained by questions;
- the town beginning to choose sides;
- the killer quietly feeding the sleuth a false interpretation.

The pressure should arise from what the sleuth has done or discovered.

## 12. Build a Persuasive First Theory
<!-- job: persuasive-first-theory -->

By late Act II, the protagonist should believe they are beginning to understand the case.

The first theory should be:

- supported by several real clues;
- emotionally persuasive;
- capable of explaining at least part of the crime;
- wrong, incomplete, or aimed at the wrong person;
- strong enough that its collapse matters.

A weak first theory produces a weak midpoint because there is nothing substantial to
reorient.

## 13. Midpoint Turning Point — The Case Changes Meaning
<!-- job: midpoint-case-changes-meaning -->

The midpoint should force a major reinterpretation.

The change may involve:

- the prime suspect being cleared;
- an alibi unexpectedly collapsing or holding;
- the apparent motive being exposed as a cover;
- a hidden relationship;
- a second crime or attack;
- an old event becoming relevant;
- the victim's role reversing from target to manipulator or investigator;
- the murder weapon or key object acquiring a different meaning;
- the sleuth's business, home, or identity becoming implicated;
- evidence that the murder was planned for a different reason than assumed.

The midpoint should not merely add a larger clue. It should alter the governing question.

Before the midpoint, the sleuth asks one version of the case. After it, they must ask a
new one.

The act break should feel like:

> *This is not the murder we thought we were solving.*

### Act II deliverables

By the midpoint, the story should have:

- expanded the suspect field or deepened its plausible theories;
- revealed multiple private truths;
- planted important fair-play clues;
- created a persuasive but incomplete interpretation;
- imposed consequences for investigating;
- changed the meaning or direction of the case.

### Common Act II failures

- repetitive interviews that do not change theories or relationships;
- suspects who all hide the same kind of secret;
- red herrings that contribute nothing once dismissed;
- clues introduced without interpretation or consequence;
- a first theory too weak to collapse meaningfully;
- a midpoint that is merely another clue, body, or threat;
- expansion without increasing uncertainty.

# III. Act III — Only One Story Fits

**Approximate range:** 50–75%

**Primary movement:** reinterpretation → elimination → pressure → apparent defeat

**Mystery function:** narrow the field, test competing explanations, connect evidence,
and expose the personal cost of being wrong.

**Reader question:** *Which explanation can still survive everything we now know?*

## 14. Restart the Investigation Under a New Theory
<!-- job: restart-under-new-theory -->

The midpoint should change how the sleuth reads earlier information.

Act III often revisits:

- witnesses already questioned;
- alibis treated as settled;
- objects previously dismissed;
- the victim's final movements;
- conversations whose meaning has changed;
- assumptions inherited from police, gossip, or the sleuth's own bias.

These should not be repeated scenes. The question, stakes, knowledge, or power dynamic
must now be different.

## 15. Begin Eliminating False Stories
<!-- job: eliminate-false-stories -->

Act III is the contraction phase.

Suspects and theories should be cleared, weakened, or separated from the murder through
new interpretation:

- a lie concealed an affair rather than murder;
- missing money explains fear but not opportunity;
- a false alibi protected someone else;
- a suspicious object belonged to another secret;
- hostility toward the victim masked an act of protection;
- apparent motive existed, but the method or timeline does not fit.

Each eliminated theory should leave behind something useful: a fact, relationship,
constraint, motive refinement, or clue pointing toward the remaining explanation.

## 16. Reveal the Deeper Motive Structure
<!-- job: deeper-motive-structure -->

The murder should begin to feel emotionally and causally inevitable in retrospect.

The apparent motive may have involved money, romance, inheritance, rivalry, or revenge.
The deeper motive often involves:

- shame;
- identity;
- fear of exposure;
- protection of family or status;
- an old betrayal;
- abandonment;
- control;
- a life constructed around deception;
- the need to preserve a cherished public story.

This deeper layer should connect the murder to the book's character and community themes,
not merely provide a more dramatic confession.

## 17. Let the Killer Shape the Investigation
<!-- job: killer-shapes-investigation -->

By Act III, the antagonistic force should no longer feel passive.

The killer may:

- destroy, clean, move, or manufacture evidence;
- pressure a witness;
- feed the sleuth a false theory;
- frame another suspect;
- exploit gossip;
- threaten the sleuth's livelihood or belonging;
- manipulate police assumptions;
- provoke conflict among allies;
- attempt to make the case appear closed.

The killer need not become physically violent. What matters is that they respond
intelligently to danger and try to preserve their version of events.

## 18. Make the Investigation Personally Costly
<!-- job: personally-costly -->

The sleuth's pursuit of truth should now threaten something established in Act I:

- community acceptance;
- a business or vocation;
- a friendship;
- a romantic possibility;
- family trust;
- a sanctuary;
- confidence in their own judgement;
- belief in someone they care about.

The cost should result partly from the sleuth's choices. A protagonist who suffers only
because the killer attacks them remains emotionally passive.

## 19. Create a Second Pressure Point
<!-- job: second-pressure-point -->

Near the later part of Act III, the killer or the false solution appears to regain
control.

Possible pressure points include:

- a persuasive arrest of the wrong person;
- a false confession;
- the key witness disappearing;
- decisive evidence being discredited;
- the sleuth discovering that a trusted ally lied;
- the remaining suspect appearing impossible;
- the protagonist being publicly blamed;
- the police formally closing the case;
- a trap or proof test failing.

This beat should not merely delay the answer. It should expose the flaw in the sleuth's
current reasoning.

## 20. Converge the Clues
<!-- job: converge-clues -->

Separate details should begin forming one pattern.

The sleuth may not yet understand the whole solution, but the story should show that:

- several clues point to the same hidden fact;
- the remaining timeline is narrowing;
- one suspect's knowledge or behaviour is becoming difficult to explain;
- the killer's lie requires increasingly strained assumptions;
- earlier red herrings have removed alternative explanations.

The reader should feel the mystery tightening.

## 21. Act III Turning Point — Apparent Defeat and Final Contradiction
<!-- job: act-iii-apparent-defeat -->

The sleuth's preferred answer should collapse, become unprovable, or cause meaningful
harm.

The low point may involve:

- the prime suspect being conclusively cleared;
- the wrong person being arrested;
- an innocent person being damaged by the sleuth's accusation;
- the town withdrawing trust;
- the sleuth quitting or being excluded;
- crucial evidence appearing worthless;
- the killer successfully framing someone;
- the sleuth realising their central assumption was wrong.

Yet one fact still refuses to fit.

That surviving contradiction should carry the story into Act IV. It must already exist in
the narrative and become newly important because the false solution has failed.

The act break should answer:

> *What single unresolved fact prevents the case from being truly closed?*

### Act III deliverables

By the transition into Act IV, the story should have:

- reinterpreted earlier evidence;
- eliminated or weakened major false theories;
- narrowed the suspect field;
- exposed the deeper motive structure;
- shown the killer actively protecting the lie;
- imposed a personal cost on the sleuth;
- collapsed the apparent solution;
- preserved one decisive contradiction.

### Common Act III failures

- continuing to add suspects rather than narrow them;
- repeated interviews with no changed interpretation;
- red herrings resolved only during the final explanation;
- a passive killer who waits to be discovered;
- danger unrelated to the sleuth's investigation;
- no personal consequence for being wrong;
- a low point caused only by external bad luck;
- no surviving contradiction to trigger the endgame.

# IV. Act IV — The Truth Restores Order

**Approximate range:** 75–100%

**Primary movement:** recognition → verification → exposure → restoration

**Mystery function:** convert the final contradiction into a complete explanation, prove
it, expose the killer, and make the truth public.

**Reader question:** *How do the clues form one unavoidable answer, and what does that
truth do to the community?*

## 22. Trigger the Epiphany Through Established Knowledge
<!-- job: epiphany-from-established-knowledge -->

The sleuth sees the decisive pattern because ordinary life gives the final contradiction
new meaning.

The epiphany may arise through:

- repeating a craft process;
- noticing pressure, residue, timing, balance, damage, or repair;
- hearing familiar language used incorrectly;
- observing a routine performed in the wrong order;
- recognising a social habit;
- revisiting an object or statement under the new theory;
- understanding what a suspect was emotionally protecting.

The epiphany should connect established clues. It should not introduce the missing answer
through sudden memory, coincidence, or new evidence.

## 23. Reconstruct the True Story
<!-- job: reconstruct-true-story -->

The sleuth should be able to explain:

- what the killer wanted;
- why the victim became an obstacle;
- what happened before, during, and after the murder;
- how the killer created or exploited misleading evidence;
- why the initial theories appeared convincing;
- what the decisive contradiction actually meant;
- how the killer responded as the investigation advanced.

The reconstruction should account for the whole case, not merely identify the culprit.

## 24. Solve the Proof Problem
<!-- job: solve-proof-problem -->

Private certainty is not enough. The sleuth needs a way to verify or expose the truth.

The proof phase may involve:

- confirming a timeline;
- testing an object or material fact;
- recovering evidence already established;
- inducing the killer to reveal forbidden knowledge;
- demonstrating that an alibi depends on a false premise;
- protecting the person being framed;
- arranging witnesses so contradictory stories collide;
- persuading police to test a specific claim.

The protagonist should act deliberately. The endgame should not depend solely on the
killer conveniently confessing.

## 25. Force the Final Action
<!-- job: force-final-action -->

The sleuth may confront the killer, lay a trap, announce false information, recreate part
of the crime, protect a likely target, or bring competing stories into public conflict.

The action should be risky but plausible. The protagonist should not behave recklessly
merely because the plot requires a confrontation.

The strongest final action grows from:

- the sleuth's established skills;
- available allies and community spaces;
- the proof problem;
- the killer's specific fear or need;
- the emotional conflict at the heart of the motive.

## 26. Expose the Killer
<!-- job: expose-killer -->

The climax should break the killer's controlling story.

The killer may:

- contradict established evidence;
- reveal knowledge they should not possess;
- attempt to destroy proof;
- turn on the framed suspect;
- flee;
- confess under emotional pressure;
- make one final attempt to manipulate the sleuth or community.

The solution should feel:

- surprising in the moment;
- logical in retrospect;
- grounded in clues already visible;
- specific to this protagonist and this world;
- emotionally connected to the deeper motive.

## 27. Allow Official and Social Truth to Land
<!-- job: official-and-social-truth -->

After exposure, the story should clarify what becomes provable, publicly known, legally
actionable, and emotionally accepted.

These may not be identical.

Possible outcomes include:

- arrest;
- confession;
- formal exposure;
- prevention of further harm;
- legal uncertainty but social truth;
- one crime solved while a broader secret survives;
- a killer removed from the community without every consequence becoming public.

The ending should remain faithful to the tone and moral design of the series.

## 28. Restore the World
<!-- job: restore-world -->

The denouement should show the community absorbing the truth.

Restore through concrete life rather than summary alone:

- reopen the shop or studio;
- return to a meal, class, market, festival, or routine;
- clear innocent suspects;
- repair or redefine relationships;
- allow the victim to be remembered more honestly;
- show how the sleuth's place in the community has changed;
- advance a romantic, family, or friendship thread;
- end with a light series-facing coda.

The world should not return to its exact previous state. The truth has changed it.

The final promise is:

> *This place has been wounded, but it is safe, connected, and pleasurable enough to
> revisit.*

### Act IV deliverables

By the end of the novel, the story should have:

- connected established clues into one explanation;
- distinguished insight from proof;
- given the sleuth an intentional endgame action;
- exposed the killer through earned logic;
- clarified the consequences of the truth;
- restored community, relationship, and reader comfort.

### Common Act IV failures

- a sudden clue solving the case;
- a confession replacing deduction;
- the sleuth naming the killer without proving anything;
- a confrontation unrelated to the protagonist's skills or arc;
- the police arriving through coincidence;
- an explanation that ignores major clues or red herrings;
- legal resolution without social aftermath;
- ending immediately after arrest;
- restoration reduced to a brief paragraph.

# V. Act Transitions at a Glance

Each act should end because the story's governing question changes.

## Act I → Act II

**From:** *What happened?*

**To:** *Which of these people and hidden conflicts explains it?*

**Required change:** the sleuth commits to active investigation.

## Act II → Act III

**From:** *Which plausible theory is correct?*

**To:** *What if the case means something fundamentally different?*

**Required change:** the midpoint destroys or transforms the first understanding.

## Act III → Act IV

**From:** *Which explanation survives the evidence?*

**To:** *What does the final contradiction prove, and how can it be exposed?*

**Required change:** the apparent solution fails, leaving one decisive unresolved fact.

## Act IV → Resolution

**From:** *Can the sleuth prove the truth?*

**To:** *How does the community live with it?*

**Required change:** private understanding becomes public consequence and restoration.

# VI. Information Rhythm

A mystery's four acts also control what the reader believes.

## Act I — A question exists

The reader knows there is a crime, an enclosed social world, and an initial contradiction.

## Act II — Many answers could be true

The reader gains information while uncertainty grows. Several suspects and motives appear
capable of explaining the murder.

## Act III — Most answers cannot be true

New interpretation eliminates possibilities. The case becomes narrower, more personal,
and harder to avoid.

## Act IV — One answer must be true

The surviving contradiction connects the clues into a complete and provable explanation.

A useful diagnostic is:

> If Act II does not widen uncertainty, it is not truly expanding. If Act III does not
> reduce uncertainty, it is not truly contracting.

# VII. Loose Four-Act Beat Sequence

These are common structural functions, not mandatory chapters. Several may share a scene,
and a single function may span multiple chapters.

## Act I — Disruption and Commitment

1. Establish the protagonist's ordinary world and social position.
2. Show the protagonist's distinctive expertise or way of noticing.
3. Introduce the victim, tensions, or an early disturbance.
4. Discover or learn of the murder.
5. Establish the official response and first contradiction.
6. Introduce the initial suspect field.
7. Give the sleuth a personal reason to act.
8. End with active commitment to investigate.

## Act II — Expansion and Reorientation

9. Begin informal enquiries through ordinary life.
10. Develop the first plausible suspect theory.
11. Reveal a private secret mistaken for guilt.
12. Plant or reinforce a fair-play clue under a false interpretation.
13. Let investigation create social consequence.
14. Expand the victim's hidden life and suspect motives.
15. Build a persuasive first explanation.
16. Change the meaning of the case at the midpoint.

## Act III — Contraction and Apparent Defeat

17. Reinvestigate earlier facts under the new theory.
18. Eliminate one false suspect or explanation.
19. Reveal the deeper motive structure.
20. Let the killer manipulate evidence, people, or perception.
21. Increase the personal cost of being wrong.
22. Narrow the field and converge the clues.
23. Collapse the apparent solution or produce a damaging false answer.
24. End with one contradiction that still does not fit.

## Act IV — Proof, Exposure, and Restoration

25. Let ordinary expertise reveal the meaning of the contradiction.
26. Reconstruct the true chronology and motive.
27. Identify what must be proved.
28. Test the theory or create a controlled exposure.
29. Confront the killer's final lie.
30. Allow official or public truth to land.
31. Show consequences for victim, suspects, and relationships.
32. Restore community, vocation, belonging, and series continuity.

> **A beat is not a chapter.** The structure describes changes in story function, not a
> fixed chapter count. Mapping one beat onto one chapter produces artificial pacing and
> makes books in a series feel structurally cloned.

# VIII. Use in an AI Review Pass

Use this framework as a **macro-structure pass**, separate from the cozy-archetype,
murder-logic, character, and scene-execution passes.

The reviewer should:

1. identify only material structural weaknesses;
2. locate the affected act, transition, beat, or chapter range;
3. explain the consequence for mystery momentum or reader understanding;
4. distinguish a missing function from an optional structural variation;
5. recommend the smallest story-specific revision that repairs the movement;
6. avoid equal-weight scoring or demanding exact percentages;
7. avoid reviewing prose, tone, coziness, or forensic logic unless they directly damage
   an act function.

## Priority questions

- Does Act I end with a genuine investigative commitment?
- Does Act II expand the number or plausibility of possible explanations?
- Is the first theory persuasive enough to matter?
- Does the midpoint change the governing question of the case?
- Does Act III narrow rather than continue expanding?
- Are false theories eliminated through evidence and reinterpretation?
- Does the killer actively protect the lie?
- Does Act III end with apparent defeat and a surviving contradiction?
- Does Act IV distinguish epiphany, verification, exposure, and restoration?
- Does each act transition create a new mode of story rather than merely provide more
  information?

## Common structural diagnoses

- **Late commitment:** the protagonist reacts for too long before choosing to investigate.
- **Flat expansion:** Act II adds scenes but not credible alternative explanations.
- **Decorative midpoint:** the midpoint adds danger or information without changing the
  case's meaning.
- **Endless expansion:** new suspects and secrets continue appearing deep into Act III.
- **Weak contraction:** theories disappear through convenience rather than testing.
- **Passive antagonist:** the killer does nothing to preserve the false story.
- **False low point:** the sleuth is delayed but not meaningfully wrong, damaged, or
  excluded.
- **Unprepared epiphany:** the answer arrives through new information rather than changed
  interpretation.
- **Missing proof phase:** the sleuth understands the answer and immediately confronts the
  killer.
- **Abrupt restoration:** the book resolves the crime but not the social world.

## Recommended output format for the AI reviewer

For each material issue, return:

- **Structural location:** act and relevant beats or chapters;
- **Diagnosis:** the missing or weak function;
- **Reader consequence:** what becomes flat, confusing, repetitive, or unearned;
- **Evidence:** specific outline events supporting the diagnosis;
- **Minimal repair:** the smallest revision that restores the act movement;
- **Downstream effects:** later beats that must change if the repair is adopted.

The review should prioritise no more than five structural issues unless the outline is
fundamentally incomplete.

# IX. Relationship to Other Development Passes

This structure should not absorb every story concern.

## Four-act pass

Checks:

- macro movement;
- act direction;
- expansion and contraction;
- midpoint reorientation;
- low point and final contradiction;
- proof and restoration sequence.

## Cozy-archetype pass

Checks:

- genre promise;
- world and community;
- amateur access;
- suspect humanity;
- cozy-safe escalation;
- ordinary-world expertise;
- belonging and emotional restoration.

## Murder-logic pass

Checks:

- killer chronology;
- motive, means, and opportunity;
- clue validity;
- alibis and timelines;
- fair-play disclosure;
- causal behaviour before and after the murder.

## Scene-execution pass

Checks:

- scene purpose;
- information delivery;
- pacing and repetition;
- emotional continuity;
- viewpoint and chapter-level causality.

The passes should remain diagnostically separate and be combined only in the final
revision plan.

# Four-Act Structure in One Sentence

A murder wounds an intimate world, the amateur sleuth expands the case into competing
stories, then narrows those stories through pressure and reinterpretation until one
contradiction reveals a provable truth and allows the community to be restored.
